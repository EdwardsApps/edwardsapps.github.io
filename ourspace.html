The problem

Did you book that?

Most households don't fall apart over big things. They fall apart over a dentist appointment nobody wrote down, a bin day when nobody can remember whose turn it is, and a direct debit neither of you can account for. The tools that exist for this were mostly built for offices: a shared calendar assumes you both work the same week, a task app assumes somebody is the project manager, and a budgeting app assumes one person owns the money.

OurSpace is built on the household's assumptions instead: both of you see the same day, the same lists and the same money, and the app does the remembering.

What it does

The household admin, handled together.

Shared to-do lists that both of you can see and tick off. A calendar that shows the whole household's week, not just one person's. Budgets and money overviews you can talk about over the same screen. In full:

  • Both calendars in one view: each of you connects your own Google Calendar (read-only — OurSpace can look, never change anything) and the events appear side by side. You can add events inside OurSpace too, which both of you see.
  • Shared tasks: due dates, priorities, who's doing it, and a place on the map so the errand can be found as well as remembered.
  • One shopping list: live on both phones, with quantities, who's picking it up, and quick search links to Amazon or your own supermarket. Tell it which shop you're heading to and it sorts itself into aisle order, then learns the way round your branch from the order you tick things off.
  • Dinner plans: an AI meal planner drafts the week's dinners around your tastes; accept the ones you fancy and the ingredients land on the shopping list in a tap.
  • A recipe library: save the dinners you'd happily eat again, and future weeks are drafted around your own favourites first, using your ingredients and steps exactly as you saved them.
  • A household handbook: the Wi-Fi password, how the front door works, where the stopcock is, who to ring — written down once, and shareable with a grandparent, sitter or guest as a read-only link that expires when you say.
  • Budgets and money: accounts, direct debits, debts, pensions and assets, totalled into one net-worth figure.
  • Setting up without the typing: download a ready-made spreadsheet, fill in what you already have, and upload it — accounts, assets, direct debits and contracts all land at once.
  • Or hand it a statement: upload a bank statement PDF and OurSpace reads off the closing balance and the payments that look regular, then asks you to approve each one before anything changes.
  • Household contracts: insurance, utilities, broadband and the rest, each with its renewal date, account details and notes, and a reminder in the app before it rolls over.
  • Bin days: tell it your collection rotation once (weekly, fortnightly or monthly) and the app shows which bins go out, the day before and on the day.
  • Birthdays: pulled from your Google Calendar, with a week's notice and gift ideas on tap.
  • The kids' week (optional): each child's school days, clubs and routines, with who's dropping off and who's collecting.
  • A noticeboard for the two of you: sticky notes, doodles and links, each clearing itself after 24 hours.
  • Catch Up: a short digest, written for you each time you open the app, of what your partner has added or ticked off since you last looked.
  • Your own AI assistant, if you want one: connect Claude, ChatGPT or Mistral’s Le Chat, and ask it about the household instead of opening the app. It's optional, it stays off until you set it up, and the connection guide above explains how.

OurSpace also runs in the browser at our-space.me, and installs straight to your home screen from there. The stores are the easy route; the web app is the same thing.

What it costs

£2.99 inc VAT once, and not paid for with your data.

OurSpace is Lifetime Premium: a one-off £2.99 payment including VAT for the whole household. On iPhone, payment is handled by the App Store. On Android and on the web, users sign in and pay through the Stripe checkout. There is no free trial, no subscription, no advert in the corner, and no paid tier hiding behind a free one.

It isn't funded by advertising, and nothing about your household is sold to anyone. The AI features we build in each get a deliberately narrow slice and no more: the Catch Up digest sees the names of things and who they're for, never your balances, locations or calendar events. The one exception is the optional statement reader: it passes the PDF you give it to Google Gemini to be read, and honestly, we can't promise you that Google or the platform underneath us never retains or trains on it — so it only runs on a file you choose to upload, and it changes nothing until you approve each suggestion. Those narrow limits are limits on what OurSpace sends by itself, and they don't hold for an AI assistant you connect yourself, which can fetch any of it when you ask (that's described further up the page, and set out in full in the privacy policy). Google Calendar is connected read-only, so OurSpace can show your diary but never alter it. The household handbook is the one thing you can choose to show someone outside the household, and even then only the entries you've marked as shareable, only for as long as the link lasts, and never to an AI. Link with your partner when you're ready and unlink any time; deleting your account removes the lot. The full detail is in the privacy policy.

The details

What you notice after a week.

The screens are the easy part. The point of OurSpace is the small connections it makes on your behalf, so less rests on one person's memory.

  • Catch up in ten seconds

    Open the app and a short AI-written digest covers what changed while you were away, so you're already up to date before anyone has to ask.

  • Dinner, decided

    Tell it your tastes once and the meal planner drafts a week of dinners, with sides if you want them and step-by-step cooking instructions. Accept the ones you fancy, swap any day, and every ingredient lands on the shared shopping list. Save the winners to your recipe library and the next plan starts from them — favourites first, then whatever you haven't had for a while — before it reaches for new ideas.

  • A handbook for whoever's minding the house

    Every household has the same ten questions from a sitter or a visiting parent: the Wi-Fi, the key safe, the stopcock, the bins, the vet. Write each one down once, mark which ones a guest may see, and hand them a link that opens in any browser with no app and no sign-in. It shows the next bin day worked out for them, counts how often it's been opened, and stops working the moment you switch it off.

  • Birthdays with the work done

    A week's warning, the age they're turning (once you've told it the year), and an AI-written gift idea with a link straight to Amazon. Mark a birthday as a surprise and it hides from your partner; it's the only thing in the app that does.

  • Renewals that join up

    Give a contract its monthly cost and payment day and OurSpace creates the matching direct debit, ready to pull into the budget in one tap. The renewal is flagged in the app ahead of time, with a price-comparison link where one exists.

  • The setting-up problem, solved

    Money apps ask you to type in a life before they're any use, which is where most people stop. OurSpace gives you a formatted spreadsheet to fill in instead — or you hand it a bank statement PDF and it reads the balance and the payments that repeat, then puts them in front of you as a list to approve. Nothing is saved until you say so.

  • Errands that group themselves

    Tasks carry a place as well as a date. Two jobs near each other are flagged as one trip, and when a calendar event has a location, the tasks you could do on the way are shown beside it.

  • A board that tidies itself

    Pin a sticky note, a doodle drawn with a finger, or a link for later. Your partner gets a nudge on their phone, and the board clears itself after 24 hours; little moments rather than clutter.

  • Notifications you choose

    When your partner adds a task or a shopping item, your phone gets a push notification. Which kinds you get is up to each of you, in Settings.

  • Built for two

    Link accounts with a six-character couple code and you both see the same household from then on. There's no team, no admin and no permissions matrix; neither of you is anyone's assistant.

Where it comes from

Where OurSpace came from.

EdwardsApps mostly builds operational software for the construction trade, where the whole job is keeping several people working from the same information. OurSpace came from noticing that a household faces exactly the same problem (two people acting on slightly different versions of the week) with none of the tools to meet it.

It's made in the UK: designed and built in Buckinghamshire, priced in pounds, with bin rotations and renewal reminders that fit how British households actually run, and supported by the person who wrote it.

It's actively developed, and most of what gets built next comes from households saying where it doesn't fit. If your week works differently from an assumption in the app, that's worth an email; it usually means the assumption is wrong.
